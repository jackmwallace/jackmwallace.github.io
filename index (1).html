How a page got made

Every letter is a small block of metal called a sort, cast with its face reversed. The compositor sets sorts into a line, the line is built into a page, and the page is locked into an iron frame. That frame, with the type in it, is the forme. Ink goes on the raised faces, damp paper is pressed against them, and the reversed letters print the right way round.

Start at the case. A half-finished line is already waiting in your stick. At the stone you can also choose folio, quarto or octavo, then fold the printed sheet into a book.

At the case

The compositor holds the stick in the left hand and sets each sort with its nick up, so the line runs left to right but upside down, and every face is a mirror image. Here the sorts show exactly that. The proof line shows what the finished print will say.

Boxes are sized by use, so the e is large and the z small. Capitals sit in the upper case, which is how it got its name. J and U sit after Z because they were still thought of as forms of I and V. The long s (ſ) was used at the start and middle of words. The lay here is simplified from Moxon's, 1683.

A line must fit the measure exactly, or the type falls apart when lifted. Fill the gap with quads and spaces; a slip of paper makes up the last point. In a real shop each letter came in a limited number, and running short left you out of sorts. Here every box is unlimited.

At the stone

The stone gives a perfectly flat surface. Pages are set in an iron chase, wooden furniture fills the gaps, and quoins, which are wedges, are driven in until everything is tight. A planer is tapped across the faces to bring them level. Only then can the forme be lifted as a single piece.

Books were printed on whole sheets carrying several pages each. Imposition means arranging the pages so that, once the sheet is folded, they fall in order. Each sheet needs two formes, one for each side. Choose folio, quarto or octavo above to see the layouts. Pages in the top row of a quarto or octavo are set upside down, and page numbers are set for you.

Print both formes on the same sheets, then open the paper pile and fold one.

    At the press

    The pressman beats ink, a mix of linseed-oil varnish and lampblack, onto two leather balls and dabs it over the type. Paper is dampened the day before so it takes the ink. It goes on the tympan, the frisket folds down to keep the margins clean, and the whole carriage is wound in on rails by the rounce.

    The platen is smaller than the bed, so a forme took two pulls of the bar. The wet sheet then hung up to dry. An experienced crew is often said to have managed about 200 to 250 impressions an hour, with one person inking while the other pulled.

    For a folded format, one side of every sheet is printed from the outer forme and the sheets are then turned and printed from the inner forme. Small holes pricked by points on the tympan keep the two sides in register.
