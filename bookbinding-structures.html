1. Periods

Dates are approximate and practice varied by region and by the price of the binding. The structure table follows the order in which a binder works: sewing first, covering and decoration last.

2. The sewing frame

Sewing holds the quires together, and in most Western structures it also joins the text block to the boards. Choose a sewing method, then sew quire by quire. The spine view looks at the backs of the folds, with the head of the book on the left. Thread inside a fold is dashed; thread on the outside of the spine is solid.

3. From quires to bound book

Binders divide the work into forwarding (sewing, shaping and covering) and finishing (decoration). Choose a structure and step through it. The large drawing is a section through the head of the book; the smaller one shows the outside.

    Sources

    Main references

    • Philippa Marks, The British Library Guide to Bookbinding: History and Techniques (1998). A good short introduction.
    • David Pearson, English Bookbinding Styles 1450–1800 (2005). The best practical guide to dating and describing ordinary trade bindings.
    • Mirjam Foot, The History of Bookbinding as a Mirror of Society (1998), and her collected Studies in the History of Bookbinding.
    • Howard Nixon and Mirjam Foot, The History of Decorated Bookbinding in England (1992).

    Further reading

    • Bernard Middleton, A History of English Craft Bookbinding Technique (1963, later revised). The standard account of English technique from the 16th century onward.
    • E. P. Goldschmidt, Gothic & Renaissance Bookbindings (1928).
    • Anthony Hobson, Humanists and Bookbinders (1989). Italian Renaissance gold-tooling and its spread.
    • Douglas Ball, Victorian Publishers' Bindings (1985). The shift to cloth and edition binding.
    • Matt Roberts and Don Etherington, Bookbinding and the Conservation of Books: A Dictionary of Descriptive Terminology (1982), online at cool.culturalheritage.org/don.
    • Princeton University Library, Hand Bookbinding exhibition, on early and later sewing.
    • Ligatus, Language of Bindings thesaurus, for standard terms.
    • See also this Introduction to Bookbinding Exhibit
    The diagrams are teaching schematics, not measured drawings: proportions are exaggerated so the thread, supports and boards can be seen. Dates summarise the standard accounts above and should be checked against them for any particular binding.
