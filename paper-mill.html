2. The mould

Paper is made by lifting fibres suspended in water onto a screen. The European hand mould, developed in Italy, is a rigid wooden frame covered with wires. Everything you see in a sheet held up to the light comes from it. Choose a mould, then select a part to find it on the drawing.

    4. The paper machine

    Nicolas-Louis Robert patented a machine for making paper in a continuous web in France in 1799. Developed in England with the backing of the stationers Henry and Sealy Fourdrinier and built by Bryan Donkin, it was working at Frogmore Mill in Hertfordshire by 1803. Watch the web form, and change the dandy roll to see how machine-made laid and wove paper differ.

    6. Paper as evidence

    Because the chain lines and watermark sit in fixed places on the sheet, folding moves them in predictable ways. This is the standard way of finding the format of a hand-press book, as set out in Gaskell. Choose a format to see where the evidence falls on a single leaf.

    Dating by watermarks

    • Moulds were made and used in pairs (twins), one being couched while the vatman formed a sheet on the other. A stock of paper therefore carries two near-identical marks, which can be told apart by small differences in the wire.
    • Moulds wore out after a few years of hard use. Paper made on the same pair of moulds was therefore made within a short span. Allan Stevenson showed that matching twin marks in an undated book against dated documents can date it closely. His case study is The Problem of the Missale Speciale (London: Bibliographical Society, 1967). The undated Missale speciale, or Constance Missal, had been claimed as older than the Gutenberg Bible; by tracing its watermarks to paper in dated documents, he placed its printing in the early 1470s.
    • Marks are identified by tracing or photographing them and comparing them with the repertories: Briquet (to 1600), Piccard (now searchable online as Piccard-Online), Churchill and Heawood (seventeenth and eighteenth centuries). A similar mark gives only an approximate date; an identical one, from the same mould, a close one.
    • From the 1790s many English papers carry a date in the watermark. It gives the earliest possible date of printing, not the date itself, because paper could lie in stock for years.

    Sources

    Papermaking

    • Dard Hunter, Papermaking: The History and Technique of an Ancient Craft (1943; 2nd ed. 1947). The classic starting point; dated in places but still indispensable.
    • Jérôme de Lalande, L'art de faire le papier (1761), in the French Academy's Descriptions des arts et métiers. The key primary source.
    • Timothy Barrett, European Hand Papermaking: Traditions, Tools, and Techniques (2018). The modern standard, informed by his research on historical paper chemistry.
    • Sylvia Rodgers Albro, Fabriano: City of Medieval and Renaissance Papermaking (2016).
    • Orietta Da Rold, Paper in Medieval England (2020).
    • Leonard Rosenband, Papermaking in Eighteenth-Century France (2000). Labour and the trade, centred on the Montgolfier mill.

    National industry histories

    • A. H. Shorter, Paper Mills and Paper Makers in England, 1495–1800 (1957).
    • D. C. Coleman, The British Paper Industry, 1495–1860 (1958).
    • Richard L. Hills, Papermaking in Britain, 1488–1988 (1988).
    • John Bidwell, American Paper Mills, 1690–1832 (2013).

    Mechanisation and wood pulp

    • R. H. Clapperton, The Paper-making Machine (1967). The Fourdrinier and cylinder machines.
    • Judith McGaw, Most Wonderful Machine (1987). Mechanisation and social change in the Berkshire paper industry.
    • Cathleen Baker, From the Hand to the Machine: Nineteenth-Century American Paper and Mediums (2010).

    Paper as evidence in printed books

    • Philip Gaskell, A New Introduction to Bibliography (1972). Its paper chapter is the standard account of how paper relates to printing practice.
    • Allan Stevenson, "Paper as Bibliographical Evidence", The Library (1962), and The Problem of the Missale Speciale (1967). These established watermark analysis as a dating method.

    Watermark repertories

    • C. M. Briquet, Les Filigranes (1907). Roughly 1282–1600.
    • W. A. Churchill, Watermarks in Paper (1935). The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
    • Edward Heawood, Watermarks, Mainly of the 17th and 18th Centuries (1950).
    • Gerhard Piccard, Findbücher; now searchable online as Piccard-Online.

    Papyrus and parchment

    • Naphtali Lewis, Papyrus in Classical Antiquity (1974).
    • Ronald Reed, Ancient Skins, Parchments and Leathers (1972) and The Nature and Making of Parchment (1975).
    • Peter Rück (ed.), Pergament: Geschichte, Struktur, Restaurierung, Herstellung (1991).
    • Christopher de Hamel, Scribes and Illuminators (1992).
    • Bruce Holsinger, On Parchment: Animals, Archives, and the Making of Culture (2022).
    • Sarah Fiddyment et al., "Animal origin of 13th-century uterine vellum revealed using noninvasive peptide fingerprinting", PNAS (2015).

    Paper beyond Europe, and its culture
